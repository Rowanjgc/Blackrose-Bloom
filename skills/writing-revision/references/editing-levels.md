# Editing levels and document structures

## What each level changes

| Level | Changes | Leaves alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Developmental | Thesis, argument, scope, section order, what to add or cut | Sentence-level polish (comes later) |
| Structural | Order of sections and paragraphs, headings, transitions | Claims and voice |
| Line edit | Sentence clarity, rhythm, concision, word choice, paragraph flow | Structure, claims, overall voice |
| Copyedit | Grammar, spelling, punctuation, consistency, terminology | Style choices that are correct |
| Proofread | Remaining typos and formatting slips | Everything else |

When levels are mixed in a request ("tighten this and fix the structure"), do
the larger one first; polishing sentences that will be cut wastes the user's time.

## Voice checklist

Before returning an edit, compare a few sentences against the original:

- Would the author plausibly have written this sentence?
- Are their characteristic words and constructions still present?
- Did formality, warmth or humor shift without a request?
- Did any hedge become a certainty, or a certainty become a hedge?

## From notes to a draft

1. Group the notes by idea, not by the order they were written.
2. Find the point the notes are converging on and state it early.
3. Order the groups so each depends only on what came before.
4. Draft connective reasoning between groups; mark where a note implies a claim
   the notes do not support.
5. Keep the author's best phrases from the notes verbatim where they fit.
6. List leftover notes that did not fit, so nothing is silently dropped.

## Structures for common documents

Use these as defaults to adapt, not templates to impose.

**Essay or article:** opening that states the question or claim; body sections
that each advance one step; strongest counterargument addressed; ending that
says what follows from the argument.

**Report:** summary with the conclusion and recommendation first; context and
question; method or sources; findings; implications; limitations; next steps.

**Technical specification:** purpose and scope; background and constraints;
goals and explicit non-goals; requirements stated testably ("must", "should");
design or interface; error and edge cases; security, privacy and compatibility
considerations; open questions; acceptance or verification criteria. Keep each
requirement atomic, unambiguous and verifiable. Flag requirements that cannot be
tested or that conflict.

**Proposal:** problem and why now; proposed change; alternatives considered;
costs, risks and mitigations; decision requested.

**Letter or email:** purpose in the first lines; context only as needed; clear
request or next step; tone matched to the relationship.

## Flagging unsupported claims

Mark a claim when it:

- states a number, date, quotation or attribution with no source;
- generalizes beyond the evidence ("everyone", "always", "proves");
- conflicts with another part of the document;
- relies on a causal link that is only implied.

Offer a fix for each: narrow the claim, add a marked placeholder for a source, or
remove it. Do not supply a source from memory as if it had been checked.
